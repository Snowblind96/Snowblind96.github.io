Current Work

The Impact of Lipid Regulation in Retinal Microglia Activation and Homeostasis

My doctoral research uses the zebrafish retina to study microglial immunometabolism — how microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, regulate their lipid metabolism, and how that metabolism in turn shapes their activation state. Microglial metabolism is plastic: it shifts upon activation, and pro-inflammatory states drive lipid synthesis and intracellular lipid droplet accumulation. My work investigates how these lipid droplets form, are regulated, and impact retinal function.

The retina is an ideal region of the central nervous system in which to study microglia. It has defined, accessible layers within an enclosed space, making microglia easy to observe in vivo, and it is highly metabolic, making it a powerful system for studying the relationship between immune activation and metabolism.

Apolipoproteins & Lipid Droplets

Determining how apolipoproteins regulate microglial lipid droplets, including the role of apoc1 (an Alzheimer's-associated gene highly expressed in microglia) in both microglial and retinal lipid handling.

Apoc1 regulates lipid handling in retinal microglia; loss of apoc1 alters lipid droplet biology.

🔁

Immunometabolism & Activation

Investigating how microglial activation states and lipid metabolism influence one another, and what lipid droplet accumulation signals about cell function.

Activation and lipid metabolism form a feedback loop: inflammatory states drive lipid synthesis and droplet accumulation.

Human health relevance. Lipid metabolic dysfunction in microglia is a conserved feature across several neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, and age-related macular degeneration — and is linked to impaired phagocytosis, oxidative damage, and chronic inflammation. Understanding the lipid-metabolism microglia connection in the retina may reveal new avenues for prevention and treatment of these conditions.

About

I am a passionate biological scientist and PhD candidate at the University of Idaho within the MMBB program, working in the Mitchell Lab. My research spans a diverse range of biological systems — from working with grizzly bears to molecular studies of microglia — and is now centered on understanding these resident immune cells of the central nervous system.

The Mitchell Lab investigates the cell and molecular biology that governs microglial function during development, degeneration, injury, and regeneration, and how microglia interact with neighboring neurons and glial cells. I focus on the intersection of neuroimmunology and metabolism: how retinal microglia regulate their lipid metabolism, and how that metabolism in turn shapes their activation state. My current work is exploring lipid droplet biology in both retinal microglia and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, and the role of apolipoproteins in regulating these processes.
